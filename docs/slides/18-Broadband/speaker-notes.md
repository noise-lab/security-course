|  | **Why Broadband Belongs in This Course** | Framing slide. Broadband is **consumer-protection infrastructure**: what you can buy, what you actually get, who is left out, and whether the **measurements** that drive policy are trustworthy. Every regulator's "X% of homes have broadband" rests on a measurement someone designed, and those designs have biases. |
|  | **A 2026 Vignette: The Cliff and the Map** | **Verified 2026-07-16.** **ACP end (June 1, 2024):** [FCC ACP page](https://www.fcc.gov/acp), [CRS report IF12637 "The End of the Affordable Connectivity Program"](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12637). ~23M enrolled households; ~5M lost home internet after wind-down. **Still no federal successor** as of mid-2026 — only [Lifeline ($9.25/mo)](https://www.fcc.gov/lifeline-consumers) and a state patchwork: **New Mexico LITAP** (up to $30/mo, launched July 1, 2026) and **California LifeLine Home Broadband Pilot** (up to $30/mo, launched January 2026). **BEAD**: [NTIA BEAD landing page](https://www.ntia.gov/funding-programs/high-speed-internet-programs/broadband-equity-access-and-deployment-bead-program) and the [NTIA BEAD Progress Dashboard](https://www.ntia.gov/funding-programs/internet-all/broadband-equity-access-and-deployment-bead-program/progress-dashboard) (54 of 56 Final Proposals approved, 52 subgrant agreements signed as of July 2026); [June 6, 2025 Restructuring Policy Notice](https://www.ntia.gov/press-release/2025/secretary-lutnick-announces-restructuring-broadband-equity-access-and-deployment). First BEAD-connected household announced in Nebraska. Cold-call: what data would tell you if ACP mattered? Adoption rates, not just availability. |
|  | **Five Lessons for the Day** | The spine of the lecture. Each lesson is a slide or two. Every lesson is a reason that the "official" broadband numbers can be wrong — and wrong numbers drive billions in policy. |
|  | **"Speed" Has Many Facets** | Hammer that "speed" is marketing shorthand. A Zoom call cares about latency, jitter, and loss far more than peak throughput. [Cloudflare on "Aim for reliability, not speed"](https://blog.cloudflare.com/speed-is-what-you-make-it/) and [M-Lab's methodology page](https://www.measurementlab.net/tests/) both explain the multi-dimensional nature. |
|  | **Different Techniques Measure Different Things** | **Active vs passive**: active tests inject traffic (speed tests); passive watches real traffic without injecting any. **Single- vs multi-threaded**: NDT-style single-stream systematically under-reports capacity vs. UDP capacity or multi-threaded tests. Not noise — methodological bias. [M-Lab NDT documentation](https://www.measurementlab.net/tests/ndt/) is the canonical reference. This is why maps built on different tools disagree. |
|  | **Many Factors Limit a Client-Based Speed Test** | The number you get is the **minimum across the whole chain**. Students conflate "my speed test" with "my ISP's speed" constantly. If the phone's Wi-Fi caps at 80 Mbps, a 1 Gbps plan reads as 80. |
|  | **The Device Can Be the Bottleneck** | • Recovered from the original deck (pptx slide 6); the callout and arrows used to animate in, so walk the picture.<br><br>• Box plots of download speed by plan tier and iPhone model (50/100/200/300 Mbps tiers; iPhone 5, 5s, 6, 6s), Aug 2015 to Jan 2016.<br><br>• The 5 and 5s never exceed about 100 Mbps on any tier because they lack 802.11ac; the 6 and 6s reach 240 Mbps on the 200 and 300 tiers.<br><br>• **What to say:** the test measured the phone's radio, not the ISP; the consumer blames the wrong party and any map built on the test inherits the error.<br><br>• **Course tie-in:** Lesson 3 (the bottleneck moves) and the "garbage in" slide: a biased instrument, not a dirty row.<br><br>• The archived slide carries no citation for the dataset; present it as speed-test data by device from 2015–16 unless you recall the source. |
|  | **The Home Network Is Often the Bottleneck** | Sundaresan et al., ["Home Network or Access Link? Locating Last-Mile Downstream Throughput Bottlenecks" (PAM 2016)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-30505-9_9). Above ~35 Mbps, the access link is almost never the bottleneck. Wi-Fi contention dominates, and *grows* with faster plans. Policy implication: subsidizing a faster tier may not help if the home network is the constraint. |
|  | **Faster "Speed" Doesn't Mean Better Performance** | Page-load-time plateau ~16 Mbps. Anchor: [Sundaresan, Feamster & Teixeira, "Home Network or Access Link?"](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-30505-9_9) and the [Wall Street Journal, "You're Probably Paying for Internet Speed You Don't Need"](https://www.wsj.com/articles/youre-probably-paying-for-internet-speed-you-dont-need-1519134401). Consumer-protection punchline: ISPs upsell tiers whose experience plateaus for most everyday tasks. |
|  | **Applications Rarely Use the "Top Speed"** | • Recovered from the original deck (pptx slide 10); static scatter, both axes log scale, so narrate it.<br><br>• One point per home and app: daily peak utilization against that week's measured capacity, from the Internet Equity Initiative's in-home measurements (the same figure appears on the archive's "new system" slide as "Application Performance Measurement").<br><br>• Below about 50 Mbps the points sit on the diagonal: the link really is the limit and a faster tier would help those homes.<br><br>• Above about 100 Mbps the cloud flattens at 50 to 100 Mbps; the two homes near 600 Mbps stream at the same 50 Mbps as a 150 Mbps home.<br><br>• **What to say:** Lesson 4 in one picture; if apps never saturate the link, measure the application's experience, not peak throughput.<br><br>• **Course tie-in:** sets up the next slide (application-quality metrics) and the BEAD question of whether a gigabit upgrade moves a household anywhere it can feel. |
|  | **User Experience Lives in the Application** | Streaming/video-call quality metrics: startup delay, resolution, bitrate changes, rebuffering. Motivates passive measurement (and its privacy tradeoffs). [MacMillan et al., "Measuring the Performance and Network Utilization of Popular Video Conferencing Applications" (IMC 2021)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3487552.3487842) is a good reference for the specific facts on the "Passive Measurement Yields Real Insight" slide. |
|  | **The Divide Is Local — and Right Next Door** | The **Hyde Park vs. South Shore** comparison is the equity heart of the lecture and it's measurable. Anchor: [UChicago Internet Equity Initiative](https://internetequity.uchicago.edu/) — same 1 Gbps Comcast plan, two neighborhoods, dramatically different delivered performance. "Same service plan ≠ same performance." |
|  | **What Counts as "Broadband"?** | **2015 → 25/3 Mbps**; **March 2024 → 100/20 Mbps** in the [FCC's 2024 Section 706 Report](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-increases-broadband-speed-benchmark-0) (also set 1 Gbps / 500 Mbps as a long-term aspirational goal). **July 2025:** [Carr FCC's Fact Sheet DOC-413059A1](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-413059A1.pdf) proposes to **narrow the Section 706 inquiry to availability only** — scrap the affordability and adoption prongs, drop the 1 Gbps goal — invoking [*Loper Bright*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/22-451_7m58.pdf) to justify a "plain language" reading. Decision expected early 2026. Coverage: [Broadband Breakfast, "FCC Proposal to Scale Back Broadband Standards Draws Fire"](https://broadbandbreakfast.com/fcc-proposal-to-scale-back-broadband-standards-draws-fire/). Cleanest example of "a definition is a policy lever": move the threshold, and millions move in or out of "served." |
|  | **Bad Data: Garbage In, Garbage Out** | The data-science lesson. Sampling and methodological bias survive cleaning — you have to fix the *instrument*, not the spreadsheet. |
|  | **Limitation: Where the Tests Actually Ran** | • Embedded from the original deck's media (pptx slide 17): crowdsourced speed-test density by grid cell for Chicago, Philadelphia and Washington.<br><br>• Darker cells mean more tests: the Loop and the North Side lakefront in Chicago, Center City in Philadelphia, Northwest Washington; Chicago's South and West Sides fade to white.<br><br>• **What to say:** the map of where tests ran is not the map of where people live; the dataset "covers the city" and says least exactly where the digital-divide question is sharpest.<br><br>• **Course tie-in:** the sampling-bias half of "garbage in"; the next slide asks who the testers are, and the South Shore frame shows the alternative to accepting what the crowd produced.<br><br>• Attribution: the instructor's own figure, from the Internet Equity Initiative's crowdsourced speed-test analysis; present it as our group's work. |
|  | **Limitation: Who Runs the Test?** | Selection bias is the silent killer of broadband data. The map of "where tests ran" is not a map of "where people live." Designing a real **sampling frame** (as in the South Shore red-boundary image) is itself research. Tie back to the vignette: ACP-eligible households are under-sampled precisely because they're the least likely to run a speed test. |
|  | **Limitation: The Test Itself Lies** | M-Lab / NDT data is widely used and valuable, but the classic single-threaded implementation can under-report at high speeds. This is not a smear — [M-Lab publishes its methodology openly](https://www.measurementlab.net/publications/) and has evolved NDT (NDT7 uses BBR, TCP INFO). Teaching point: know your instrument's bias before you publish a map that moves billions. |
|  | **Where M-Lab Sits on the Curve** | • Recovered from the original deck (pptx slide 19): the "Different Techniques" CDF with the two red labels that used to appear on a click, so point to them.<br><br>• Same homes, four methods: single-threaded HTTP (the classic NDT design behind M-Lab maps) at about 0.7 of capacity, passive throughput at about 0.83, UDP capacity and multi-threaded HTTP at 0.9 to 0.95.<br><br>• The curves are parallel, so this is bias, not noise: more tests do not fix it and neither does dropping outliers.<br><br>• Underlying data: Sundaresan, De Donato, Feamster, Teixeira, Crawford and Pescapè, "Broadband Internet Performance: A View from the Gateway" (SIGCOMM 2011), the BISmark gateway deployment; cited on the archived slide 4.<br><br>• **What to say:** M-Lab publishes its methodology and NDT7 has moved on (BBR, TCP_INFO), so the lesson is humility about any instrument, not a swipe at M-Lab.<br><br>• **Course tie-in:** cold-call: a home's NDT result is 75 Mbps against a 100/20 benchmark; is it served? You cannot tell from that test alone. |
|  | **Step 1: Define the Question with Stakeholders** | Counter the Kaggle mindset. You don't start from "here's a CSV." You start from a community's actual question. [Internet Equity Initiative case studies](https://internetequity.uchicago.edu/projects/) illustrate this workflow. |
|  | **Step 2: Sometimes You Must Build the Instrument** | Router-based / in-home passive measurement. Reference implementations: [Netrics / NetMicroscope](https://internetequity.uchicago.edu/projects/nsf-netmicroscope/), [BISmark](https://projectbismark.net/), and [SamKnows](https://www.samknows.com/) (used by the FCC's Measuring Broadband America program). Cheap hardware (Raspberry Pi / Odroid), passive traffic-pattern inference, no decryption required. |
|  | **Deployment Tradeoffs: Where the Device Sits** | • Recovered from the original deck (pptx slide 32); the blue frame used to appear on a click to highlight the switch.<br><br>• Walk the chain: modem, router, switch, Wi-Fi AP with 2.4 and 5 GHz radios; the Raspberry Pi hangs off a spare switch port.<br><br>• Off-path on a switch, the Pi runs active tests (speed, latency, DNS) but is not on the path and sees nobody's packets: privacy-safe, outage-safe, blind to experience.<br><br>• In-line between modem and router (previous slide) it sees every packet and passive inference works, at the cost of a single point of failure and a much larger trust ask.<br><br>• **What to say:** placement is the privacy tradeoff made physical; the original deck followed this with "current deployment: active measurements only," which is where the Chicago pilot landed after privacy conversations with residents.<br><br>• **Course tie-in:** bridge to the privacy slide next; cold-call which placement students would accept in their own apartment. |
|  | **Step 3: Confront the Tradeoffs — Privacy** | This is where the lecture rejoins the privacy themes of the course. You can infer sleep schedules, apps in use, household size from metadata alone. Asking an under-resourced community to host a sensor demands earned trust and strong privacy guarantees. Connects to the **Surveillance/metadata** deck and **DNS** lecture. |
|  | **Passive Measurement Yields Real Insight** | Video-conferencing measurement facts. Anchor: [MacMillan et al., "Measuring the Performance and Network Utilization of Popular Video Conferencing Applications" (IMC 2021)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3487552.3487842) for the app-fairness and recovery-time findings. |
|  | **The Policy Stakes Are Live and Large** | **Affordability side (verified 2026-07-16):** ACP ended June 2024; no federal successor; NM LITAP (Jul 2026) and CA LifeLine Home Broadband Pilot (Jan 2026) are the leading state successors (up to $30/mo each); NY and CT took a different path (mandate large providers offer capped low-cost plans). **Deployment side:** [NTIA BEAD Progress Dashboard](https://www.ntia.gov/funding-programs/internet-all/broadband-equity-access-and-deployment-bead-program/progress-dashboard) — 54/56 Final Proposals approved and 52 subgrant agreements signed by mid-2026; Alabama ([$460M / 63 projects / March 2026](https://governor.alabama.gov/newsroom/2026/03/governor-ivey-announces-approval-of-final-bead-broadband-proposal-awards-460-million-to-expand-access-to-high-speed-internet/)), Mississippi ($508M, February 2026), Massachusetts ($18.8M, February 2026) are examples of active state awards. Every one of these decisions rests on a measurement someone designed. |
