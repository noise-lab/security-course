| 13 | **What Public-Key Crypto Is *Not*** | Common student misconception: "asymmetric = more secure." Kill it. Speed comparison: symmetric AES-GCM ~5–10 GB/s per core; RSA-2048 signing tens of ops/sec. The whole reason TLS uses hybrid design.<br><br><b>Case: forging 1024-bit RSA signatures through an HSM without the key (Heninger et al., Sep 20, 2026)</b><br><br>**Read first:** [Schneier on Security — "New Attack Against RSA" (Sep 28, 2026)](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2026/09/new-attack-against-rsa.html) — the two-paragraph version that says exactly what is and isn't new.<br><br>**What happened:**<br><br>• On Sep 20, 2026 Laura Shea, Miro Haller, Adam Suhl, and Nadia Heninger (UC San Diego) with Emmanuel Thomé (Inria) posted ePrint 2026/2131, "Forging 1024-bit RSA signatures in nearly SNFS time."<br>• They implemented a 2007 algorithm by Joux, Naccache, and Thomé: given temporary access to a *raw* (unpadded) RSA signing oracle, an attacker can later forge signatures on any message without ever factoring the modulus or learning the private key.<br>• The demonstration used a real hardware security module as the oracle, with FIPS mode disabled and a test key: about 1,380 CPU core-years over five months and 2^32 oracle queries, mostly precomputation; each later forgery costs about 180 core-years.<br>• Standard padded signatures (PKCS#1 v1.5, RSA-PSS) do not expose the needed oracle, so the authors say most deployments are not at immediate risk; 1024-bit RSA has been disallowed by NIST for signatures since 2013 anyway.<br>• The broader claim: with a signing oracle, RSA's concrete security is 15–30 bits below factoring-based estimates for 1024–4096-bit keys, and even 4096-bit RSA does not reach 128-bit security in that model.<br>• Status as of Oct 7, 2026: preprint, not peer-reviewed; the authors published their implementation.<br><br>**What to say:**<br><br>• "First bullet on the slide: security comes from key length and hardness assumptions, not from being asymmetric.<br>• Here is a September paper that moved the assumption a little.<br>• They sat an HSM — the box you buy so the key can never leave — on a bench, asked it to sign a few billion numbers, and afterwards could forge signatures forever without the key ever leaving.<br>• Two caveats you must give: it only works on raw, unpadded signing, which nobody should expose, and 1024-bit RSA was retired a decade ago.<br>• But the lesson is clean: the HSM kept the key perfectly and it did not matter, because the *API* leaked enough.<br>• Key management is about what the key is allowed to do, not just where it is stored."<br><br>**Course tie-in:**<br><br>• Hardness-assumption bullet on this slide; echoes §8's "conjecture, not theorem."<br>• Sets up §29's post-quantum migration: if a classical paper can shave 30 bits, a quantum computer removes the assumption entirely.<br><br>**Also:**<br><br>• [IACR ePrint 2026/2131 — Shea, Haller, Suhl, Heninger, Thomé](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2131) — the paper and abstract. |

| 18 | **Where Does Trust Stop? Trust Anchors** | Primary lists: [Mozilla CA Certificate Program](https://wiki.mozilla.org/CA) · [Chrome Root Store](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/net/data/ssl/chrome_root_store/root_store.md) · [Microsoft Trusted Root Program](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/security/trusted-root/) · [Apple Root Certificate Program](https://www.apple.com/certificateauthority/ca_program.html). a validating resolver that never picked up the new key fails closed and its users lose all DNS resolution.<br>• Resolvers that implement RFC 5011 pick the new key up automatically after a 30-day observation window; ICANN reports more than 95% of reporting resolvers had adopted it, a pace similar to the 2018 rollover.<br>• Verisign's root-server telemetry showed 91.3% of sampled resolvers trusting KSK-2024 by Mar 3, 2025, and "just shy of 100%" by its later writing; the rollover is on schedule for Sunday.<br><br>**What to say:**<br><br>• "This slide says the web's trust stops at any one of about two hundred roots.<br>• DNS went the other way: one key, one ceremony, one point of failure.<br>• That one key changes this Sunday, four days from now.<br>• If your resolver didn't learn the new key, on Sunday it doesn't get a warning — it gets nothing, for every domain.<br>• And we have a May preview of a rollover going wrong: Germany's .de registry signed its own zone badly during a routine rollover and the big public resolvers dropped the entire country's domains for hours.<br>• So: two hundred anchors or one?<br>• Which design would you rather defend, and against whom?"<br><br>**Course tie-in:**<br><br>• Trust-anchor governance on this slide, and the "public function vs. vendor" question in Breakout A: ICANN's key is run as a public ceremony, the browser root stores are run by four companies.<br>• Foreshadows the DNS-security lecture and the DNSSEC-at-issuance rule in §19.<br><br>**Also:**<br><br>• [IANA — "Updated Root KSK Rollover Guide Released" (Jul 27, 2026)](https://www.iana.org/news/2026/updated-root-ksk-rollover-guide) — the operator guidance.<br>• [Verisign — "The 2024-2026 Root Zone KSK Rollover: Initial Observations and Early Trends" (Mar 19, 2025)](https://blog.verisign.com/security/2024-2026-root-zone-ksk-rollover-initial-observations/) — the adoption telemetry.<br>• [Cloudflare — "When DNSSEC goes wrong: how we responded to the .de TLD outage" (May 6, 2026)](https://blog.cloudflare.com/de-tld-outage-dnssec/) — the May rollover failure and the serve-stale / negative-trust-anchor response. |
