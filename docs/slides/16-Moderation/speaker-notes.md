| 3 | **Online Content: Complex and at Scale** | Use a Twitch or live-stream mental image — simultaneous video, scrolling chat, sub-only modes, multiple languages. The point is scale + heterogeneity make uniform, hand-tuned moderation impossible → written policies + automation. Both get scrutinized later in the deck. |
| 4 | **Do Governments Define What Is Allowed Online?** | [47 U.S.C. §230](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230): (c)(1) immunizes platforms for third-party content; (c)(2) protects good-faith removal. EU [Digital Services Act — Commission portal](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/digital-services-act-package) · [DSA VLOP/VLOSE list, updated July 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/list-designated-vlops-and-vloses). Germany: [NetzDG — Bundesministerium der Justiz overview](https://www.bmj.de/DE/themen/heimat_integration/netzdg/netzdg_node.html) (24-hour removal duty for "clearly illegal" content; §130 StGB is the incitement statute). Don't argue one regime is better; show variety, and the consequence — in the US, private policy is the *de facto* law of online speech. |
| 5 | **The First Amendment Cuts Both Ways** | **Moody v. NetChoice, 603 U.S. 707 (July 1, 2024)** — unanimous vacate-and-remand on procedural (facial-challenge) grounds, but six justices agreed platform curation is First-Amendment-protected editorial activity: [Full opinion PDF](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/22-277_d18f.pdf) · [Justia case page](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/603/22-277/) · [CRS legal sidebar](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11224) · [Davis Wright Tremaine analysis](https://www.dwt.com/insights/2024/07/scotus-moody-ruling-a-win-for-online-platforms). Kagan wrote for the majority; Alito, Thomas, and Gorsuch called much of it "nonbinding dicta." First remand ruling: N.D. Fla. denied Florida's dismissal motion **May 22, 2025**. Teaching point: First Amendment constrains *government*, not platforms → platform policy is where most US content rules actually live. |
| 6 | **Platforms as De Facto Regulators** | Thesis slide. If private policy is the real law of online speech, systematic study of that policy is a public-interest project. Source paper: **Schaffner, Feamster et al., "Community Guidelines Make this the Best Party on the Internet: An In-Depth Study of Online Platforms' Content Moderation Policies," CHI 2024** — [ACM DL page](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642333). Live dataset browser: [ocmp43.cs.uchicago.edu](https://ocmp43.cs.uchicago.edu/). |
| 7 | **The Study: Three Topics, Three Legal Regimes** | Design logic: hold "moderation" constant, vary the legal scaffolding. Copyright has [DMCA §512](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap5.html#512). Hate speech: largely First-Amendment-protected in the US (cf. *R.A.V. v. St. Paul*, *Snyder v. Phelps*); Germany criminalizes via §130 StGB. Misinformation sits in between — only scattered laws (false advertising, market fraud, defamation). CSAM/gore intentionally out of scope by design, not importance. |
| 8 | **What Notice-and-Takedown Looks Like** | Recovered figure (original pptx slide 46): a hosting vendor's own DMCA timeline. • **Walk it left to right:** notice submitted → host vets the notice for the §512(c)(3) elements, not for merit → content removed → poster reacts → counter-notice vetted → **10-business-day** clock (§512(g)(2)(C)) → restore, or the rightsholder sues.<br><br>• **What to say:** the two dotted lines are statutory gates; the host crosses them because Congress said so, not because it judged the claim. That is why copyright policy looks uniform across 43 platforms.<br><br>• **Cold-call:** draw the same timeline for hate speech. Who sets the clock? Nobody — which is the gap the rest of the deck measures.<br><br>• **Course tie-in:** the statute is [17 U.S.C. §512](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap5.html#512); the figure is a representative vendor diagram, credited in the caption, not from the paper. |
| 9 | **Where Do We Look, and What Do We Do?** | [Tranco list](https://tranco-list.eu/) — research-grade, manipulation-resistant ranking of popular domains. 43 UGC platforms × 3 topics = 129 policy corpora. Cross-site + cross-topic breadth is the contribution over prior single-site or single-topic work. |
| 10 | **The Pipeline: Seed, Scrape, Extract, Annotate** | Recovered figure (original pptx slide 32): the four-stage pipeline from the [CHI 2024 paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642333), §4.1–4.2. • **Walk it:** seed scraper (manual: find canonical policy pages and build the keyword list) → web scraper (follow links, keep pages with keyword hits, save HTML) → process text (cut passages around hits) → annotate with the codebook.<br><br>• **What to say:** the first box is human by design because there is no standard URL for "the hate speech policy"; everything after it is repeatable, so the dataset can be rebuilt when policies change.<br><br>• **Also:** the scraper's control flow (Selenium, two-hop link following) gets its own slide later; keep this one at the level of hand-offs between stages. |
| 11 | **How We Read a Policy: The Annotation Scheme** | Walk the seven families: **Why** (justification: community values vs. legal); **What** (moderation criteria: definitions, examples, exceptions); **How found** (safeguards: user reporting vs. platform detection); **How handled** (platform response: user- vs. content-targeted, investigation); **Then what** (redress/appeal); **Who** (binding legalese/liability); and **structural** signposts. Full codebook in the [CHI 2024 paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642333). |
| 12 | **The Codebook Applied: One Annotated Passage** | Recovered figure (original pptx slide 12): one real hate-speech passage coded span by span. • **Read it aloud by colour:** orange = justification (community, trust, and safety); green = exception for critical commentary of public figures; pink = signpost ("Read More"); light blue = example sentence; dark blue = a three-prong definition (directed at a group, conveys inferiority or contempt, extremely offensive to a reasonable person).<br><br>• **What to say:** codes attach to spans, not pages, so one paragraph can carry five families. The definition here is the rare part; most of the corpus stops at the example, which is the headline finding two slides on.<br><br>• **Cold-call:** delete the dark-blue span in your head — can a user still tell what gets removed?<br><br>• **Course tie-in:** four annotators, 11,000+ segments, disagreements reconciled through the codebook in the [CHI 2024 paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3613904.3642333). |
| 13 | **A Dataset of Policy Text: OCMP-43** | Four annotators; iterative codebook; 1,300+ annotated pages, 11,000+ segments, 1.4M chars across 43 platforms × 3 topics. Dataset is open — browse it at [ocmp43.cs.uchicago.edu](https://ocmp43.cs.uchicago.edu/). Emphasize the manual effort and that this scale enables comparative questions single-site studies can't. |
| 14 | **Why Do Platforms Say They Moderate?** | Headline: justification tracks the depth of underlying law. Copyright policies overwhelmingly cite legal justifications (DMCA); where law is thin (hate speech, misinformation), platforms invoke "community values, trust, and safety." 72% of the *legal* justifications come from copyright alone. "Decency" isn't a litigable standard — platforms can't be held to it. Quote is real from Tumblr's Global Advertising Policy (accessed via OCMP-43). |
| 15 | **How Do Platforms Describe What They Moderate?** | The paper's headline finding: across all three topics, platforms lean on **examples**, not **definitions**. Examples are easy to write and flexible but leave users guessing at the boundary and give platforms discretion. Even hate speech (the most-defined) is only ~15% definitions. Quora quote from OCMP-43. |
| 16 | **How Do Platforms Find Violating Content?** | Copyright leans on automation (hashing, Content ID–style matching) because infringement is machine-detectable. Misinformation offloads to users because truth is context-dependent. Reference: [YouTube Content ID overview](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797370?hl=en) · [Facebook Rights Manager](https://rightsmanager.fb.com/). Pornhub quote from OCMP-43. |
| 17 | **Investigation and Appeal, by Topic** | Recovered figures (original pptx slides 16–17): two drawn tables. • **Numbers (approximate, from the paper's policy counts):** investigation described in ≈15% of copyright policies vs ≈85% of hate-speech and misinformation policies; appeal offered in ≈75% of copyright policies vs ≈25% of the other two.<br><br>• **What to say:** the DMCA tells a platform what to do, so it does not weigh the claim but must offer a counter-notice ([§512(g)](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap5.html#512)). With no statute, platforms describe weighing "intent and context" (Twitch) and rarely let you appeal. GitHub's "GitHub isn't the judge" line is the copyright end of the spectrum.<br><br>• **Bridge:** the next slide gives the user's side of the appeal gap in Automattic's and Fandom's own words.<br><br>• **Course tie-in:** due process tracks statute depth, the same pattern as the justification finding; ties to *Moody v. NetChoice* (states tried to mandate appeals and hit the First Amendment). |
| 18 | **Can Users Push Back?** | Fairness/due-process story. DMCA §512(g) supplies a statutory counter-notice path: [17 U.S.C. §512](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap5.html#512). Where Congress built a procedure, users have recourse; where there's no statute, the "remedy" can be "leave the platform." Connects to *NetChoice*: Texas/Florida tried to *mandate* appeals processes — exactly this gap — and ran into the First Amendment. |
| 19 | **Are Policies Even Complete?** | Only 30% (13/43) of the largest UGC platforms have policies covering all five families (justification, criteria, safeguards, response, redress). Only ~12/43 link community guidelines from the home page. Compounds two problems — incomplete *and* hard to find — undermining both user understanding and regulator oversight. Core motivation for the scraper and for machine-readable-policy proposals. |
| 20 | **Collecting the Mess: The Scraper** | ~99% success; ~100 pages needed manual collection. Constant arms race with bot-blockers. Methodological honesty: the fact that policy has to be scraped adversarially is itself evidence that policy isn't built to be read at scale. Casting a wide net over-collects on purpose; annotation filters out noise. |
| 21 | **Implications for Stakeholders** | Regulators → standardized (ideally machine-readable) policy formats so compliance can be checked (the DSA data-access provisions are the closest current instantiation). Platforms → benchmark against industry via OCMP-43. Researchers → open pipeline + dataset; extend to AI-generated content and new user types (minors, EU users under DSA Art. 28). |
| 22 | **LLMs Enter the Loop** | Manual annotation is slow; manual moderation doesn't scale → platforms increasingly use LLMs both to moderate content and to study policy. Compare against classic [Google Jigsaw Perspective API](https://perspectiveapi.com/). 2025 caveats: LLMs miss implicit toxicity, are uncertain on edge cases, and show author-identity bias. See the ICWSM 2025 "Watch Your Language" line of work and the growing literature on LLM-judge bias. Companion hands-on: [../../activities/moderation.md](../../activities/moderation.md) walks students through auditing a policy corpus. |
| 23 | **What Platforms Report vs. What They Do** | This study measures **stated** policy. The next frontier: audit enforcement. TikTok "newsworthy" carve-out shows how much discretion lives in the gap. This is exactly what the DSA transparency/data-access rules (opening vignette) are trying to make auditable — and why blocked researcher access was in the €120M X fine. |
| 24 | **The Free-Expression Tension** | Land the normative stakes. Platforms are unelected, their rules are incomplete and hidden, their enforcement is unauditable, yet they govern the *de facto* public square. **Cloudflare deplatforming arc** (Matthew Prince's own writeups): [Daily Stormer, Aug 16, 2017](https://blog.cloudflare.com/why-we-terminated-daily-stormer/) — the canonical "I woke up in a bad mood" line about unelected-arbiter risk; [8chan, Aug 5, 2019](https://blog.cloudflare.com/terminating-service-for-8chan/) after the El Paso shooting; [Kiwi Farms, Sept 3, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/kiwifarms-blocked/) after targeted harassment escalated. Context: [CJR summary](https://www.cjr.org/the_media_today/cloudflare-kiwi-farms-and-the-challenges-of-deplatforming.php) · [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2022/09/06/cloudflare-kiwi-farms-doxxing-trans-security-service/). Teaching point: the same private actor deplatforming a neo-Nazi site, a mass-shooter-adjacent forum, and a harassment forum shows there is *no* consistent, transparent rule — Prince himself calls it "dangerous." |
